Most project methods make you choose. Plan everything up front and you get control, but the plan is out of date by week three. Stay loose and you keep up with the change, but nobody can say what was agreed or what it has cost so far.

In consulting the problem usually moves while you are solving it, so I wanted both. SAFE-RUN is the framework I put together in 2025 to get them. None of the parts are new. It borrows from effectuation, which is how experienced entrepreneurs start from what they have instead of from a forecast (Sarasvathy, 2008; Fisher, 2012), from agile and lean delivery, and from ordinary consulting governance. The combination and the name are mine. It has nothing to do with SAFe, the Scaled Agile Framework.

## The one idea

**The unit of work is evidence, not tasks.** You don't promise a solution on day one. You write down what you believe, agree what you are prepared to lose finding out, and run small tests until the evidence tells you what to do next.

## Why the usual plan fails

Snowden and Boone (2007) sort the situations a leader faces into contexts, in a framework called Cynefin. Where cause and effect can be worked out, best practice or expert analysis does the job. Their complex context is different: you only understand why something worked after it has, so the sensible move is a small experiment that is safe to fail. Chaos is different again. No pattern holds, and somebody has to restore order before anything else will work.

Most consulting problems worth paying for sit in the complex one. There are layers of politics and history, and people with different stakes, so the same action lands differently every time.

I once worked with a small organisation where one person was underperforming and, it turned out, quietly recruiting the organisation's own people for a venture of his own. On the surface that is a policy problem: gather the evidence, then act. Underneath, he had a personal tie to one of the owners, the owners no longer agreed with each other, and without evidence nobody could move. The situation was sliding from complex towards chaotic, and no framework can promise a result there. It has to be steadied first.

SAFE-RUN is built for the complex case: enough structure that everyone knows the rules, enough movement that you can follow the problem when it shifts.

## SAFE: the structure

| | What you do | What you keep |
|---|---|---|
| **S**cope as hypotheses | Write the brief as statements you can test, not as fixed requirements. | A hypothesis canvas (Olsavsky, n.d.). |
| **A**ccountable guardrails | Agree up front what you can afford to lose: time, budget, reputation, ethics, safety. | A guardrail sheet the sponsor signs. |
| **F**ixed cadence | Lock a light rhythm: a daily stand-up, a weekly sponsor review, a demo every two weeks. | A cadence calendar and a RACI. |
| **E**vidence backlog | List the hypotheses with their test, metric, effort, risk, priority and owner. | The backlog itself, re-ranked weekly. |

A scope written as a hypothesis reads like this:

> We believe **[change]** will **[impact on a metric]** for **[user or process]** because **[assumption]**. We will know we are right if **[signal]** within **[time window]**.

The guardrails are the part people skip. Agreeing the affordable loss, instead of forecasting the upside, is straight from effectuation (Sarasvathy, 2008). It caps the enthusiasm, and it makes it safe to try something that might not work.

## RUN: the agility

| | What you do | What you keep |
|---|---|---|
| **R**apid probes | Run the smallest test that could prove a hypothesis wrong, in under ten days: shadow a process, pull a small slice of data. | An evidence card, a debrief note, a decision log. |
| **U**ser-side co-creation | Pair someone from the client with a consultant on every probe, starting with small commitments such as an hour of shadowing. | A partner canvas: who has committed to what. |
| **N**arrative integration | Turn the results into one rolling story the client can follow. | Situation, complication, resolution, built as a pyramid (Minto, 2009). |

Co-creation is not a courtesy. Sarasvathy (2008) calls it the patchwork quilt: you build with whoever commits, and the result takes its shape from them. It also builds trust, and trust is not a soft extra. In a study of consultants and their clients, Chalutz Ben-Gal and Tzafrir (2011) found that trust inside the pair raised the consultant's commitment to the change, and that commitment made a successful implementation more likely.

## The loop that drives it

Each probe runs through an OODA loop: observe, orient, decide, act. The idea comes from John Boyd, a US Air Force colonel and fighter pilot who developed it in the briefings he gave from 1976 onwards (Hankins, 2018). Applied to a probe:

1. **Observe**: shadow, take the data slice, run the small audit.
2. **Orient**: update the assumptions and re-rank the backlog.
3. **Decide**: scale it, kill it or park it, against the thresholds agreed in the guardrails.
4. **Act**: start the next probe or make the small change.

Then tell the client four things: what we tried, what we learned, what changes, and what we need from you.

## Running it

**Day one.** Write the hypotheses with the client and agree the guardrails. Set the cadence. Agree that a decision takes no more than 48 hours.

**Build the backlog.** List eight to twelve hypotheses that could be proved or disproved within ten days. Rank them:

**Rank = (impact × plausibility × learning speed) ÷ (effort × risk)**

Run only the top two to four at a time.

**The week.**

| When | What |
|---|---|
| Monday | Re-rank the backlog. Confirm the two to four probes. |
| Tuesday to Thursday | Run the probes. Debrief daily. |
| Friday | Team review: scale, kill or park. |
| Weekly | Sponsor review. Show only evidence and what changed. |
| Month-end | A bigger loop: stabilise, pivot, or make it permanent. |

In the reviews, keep tight timeboxes, go round the table so one voice doesn't dominate, hold each hypothesis to one metric, and give someone five minutes to argue the opposite.

## What goes wrong

- **Scope creep.** "Just one more probe." Hold the limit of two to four a week, keep the hard stops in the guardrails, and be strict about the decision log.
- **Data and privacy.** Probes touch real data. Run an ethics check on each one, anonymise what you take, take the smallest slice that answers the question, and have the sponsor sign off afterwards.
- **Politics.** When consultants start asking questions, people protect their turf. Pair them with the consultants, make their commitments visible, and credit them in the story.
- **Probes that decide nothing.** A test that ends in "interesting" is waste. Every backlog item needs one metric and a pass or fail line. If the result is unclear, park it or kill it.

## Where it doesn't fit

- **In chaos.** When nothing connects to anything, steady the situation first. SAFE-RUN starts once cause and effect can be seen again, even if only in hindsight.
- **Problems with known answers.** If best practice exists, classical project planning is faster.
- **When you can't measure.** Sometimes gathering evidence isn't possible, or isn't ethical. Then you lean on expert judgement, record it as an uncertainty, and come back when it can be measured.

## What I take from it

Balancing structure and agility is not a compromise between the two. The structure is what makes the movement safe. Liedtka (2018) makes the same point about [design thinking](/guides/design-thinking-short-version/): a process gives people enough security to stay with a problem they don't yet understand.

Because the work is counted in evidence, the method travels. Change the hypotheses, the probes and the guardrails, and the recipe stays the same from one kind of client to the next.

For me, progress came when I stopped trying to be right up front and started being explicit about what would change my mind.
