Design thinking is a way of solving problems that starts with the person who has the problem rather than with the solution you already have in mind. That sounds obvious. In practice most of us do the opposite: we see a symptom, reach for the fix we know, and ship it.

The approach came out of how designers work (Micheli et al., 2019). It's been dressed up in a lot of consultancy language since, so here is the short version.

## The one idea

**Understand the problem before you solve it, and check your understanding with the people who have it.** Everything else is scaffolding to make that happen.

A story from Christensen et al. (2016) makes the point. A builder in Detroit couldn't sell small condominiums to people downsizing from family homes. The product was fine, the price was fine, the buyers kept hesitating. When someone finally sat with them, the blocker turned out to be the dining-room table: the one the family had grown up around, which wouldn't fit. The builder didn't need a better condo; it needed to solve the table. Once it offered to move and store furniture, sales went up. No amount of analysis of the condo would have found that. Talking to the buyers did.

1. **Discover** — go wide on the problem. Watch people, interview them, look at the environment the problem lives in. Resist solving anything.
2. **Define** — narrow to the real problem, which is often not the one you were handed. Write it down. If it looks the same as the brief you started with, you probably haven't discovered enough.
3. **Develop** — go wide on solutions. Sketch, prototype, put rough versions in front of users, throw most of them away.
4. **Deliver** — narrow to the one that survived, and finish it properly.

It's drawn as a line but run as loops. You'll go back to Discover from the middle of Develop when a prototype shows you something new. That's the method working, not failing.

## The ten habits it asks of you

Micheli et al. (2019) reviewed the literature and found these attributes come up again and again. Think of them as the mindset the process needs:

| Habit | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| Creativity | Looking for an unexpected way in, not the first one. |
| User-centredness | Seeing it from the user's side, and involving them, not just studying them. |
| Problem solving | Treating it as a problem to crack, not a brief to execute. |
| Iteration | Trial, feedback, adjust, again. Small loops. |
| Collaboration across disciplines | Engineers, designers, salespeople and users in the same room. |
| Visualising | Drawing it, so people can react to something concrete. |
| Seeing the whole | The problem sits in a context; look at the context. |
| Abductive reasoning | Asking "what could be?" rather than only "what is?" |
| Tolerating ambiguity | Being comfortable not knowing for a while, and with things that don't work. |
| Blending analysis and intuition | Using the numbers and the hunch, not one or the other. |

## The eight tools

Same source. None of them is exotic:

| Tool | What it's for |
|---|---|
| Ethnography | Interviews and observation. Watching what people actually do. |
| Personas | A sketch of a typical user so the team argues about a person, not an abstraction. |
| Journey map | The user's path through the product, step by step, including how each step feels. |
| Brainstorming | Lots of ideas fast, judged later. |
| Mind map | A diagram of how the pieces relate. |
| Visualisation | Drawings, storyboards, anything that makes an idea visible. |
| Prototyping | Something cheap and rough you can put in someone's hands. |
| Experiments | Testing the solution where the problem actually lives. |

If you only remember two: talk to users, and make a rough version early.

## Where it earns its keep

- **Messy problems.** The ones with many stakeholders and no clean definition, where the data alone doesn't tell you what's wrong (Suoheimo, 2019). Design thinking gives those a structure without pretending they're tidy.
- **When you suspect you're solving the wrong problem.** The Detroit case. If a sensible fix keeps not working, the problem is probably misdefined.
- **Incremental improvement.** Small loops with users are a cheap way to keep a product moving, and they depend on people actually sharing what they learn (Gui et al., 2022).

## Where it doesn't

- **Well-understood problems with known solutions.** If the bridge needs to carry a known load, you don't need empathy interviews. You need an engineer.
- **When nobody will act on what users say.** The method only works if the organisation is willing to be wrong about its original idea. Running the workshops without that willingness produces sticky notes and nothing else.
- **As a substitute for expertise.** It helps you find the right problem. Solving it still takes people who know the domain.

## Three things that surprised me

Liedtka (2018) argues the structure is the point: it gives people enough security to stay in an uncomfortable, undefined phase instead of jumping to an answer. I'd always assumed the value was the creativity. It's more the discipline.

The iteration loops are deliberately small, so that a bad idea costs you an afternoon rather than a quarter.

And "why?", asked five times, gets you most of the way to Define on its own.
