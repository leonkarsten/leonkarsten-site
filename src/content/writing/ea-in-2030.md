After [working out what Electronic Arts was worth](/writing/what-happened-to-westwood/), I did a second piece of work on the same company, this time pointed forwards instead of back. The question was simple to ask and hard to answer: what might EA look like in 2030, and what should it be doing now?

I finished it in July 2025. On 29 September 2025 EA agreed to be bought outright for about $55 billion (Electronic Arts, 2025a). That doesn't make the exercise wrong, but it does change the answer, so I've added what I think the buyout does to each of the futures at the end. I've also fixed two things I got wrong at the time.

## How you look ahead without a crystal ball

Foresight isn't prediction. The method I used, from Hines and Bishop (2015), runs in six steps: frame the question, scan for what's changing, forecast a baseline and some alternatives, pick a preferred future, plan for it, act. I did the middle four. The point is not to be right about 2030; it's to have thought about several 2030s before one of them arrives.

The starting frame came from the Millennium Project, a think tank that tracks fifteen long-run global challenges. Challenge 14 asks how scientific and technological breakthroughs can be accelerated to improve the human condition (The Millennium Project, n.d.). EA is a technology company that happens to make games, so that was the lens.

## Fourteen forces, five that matter

Scanning means reading widely, including sources you'd normally dismiss, and sorting what you find. I sorted using STEEP (social, technological, economic, environmental, political) and came up with fourteen forces acting on EA:

- **Social:** gamer community culture; the appetite for VR and AR; human augmentation.
- **Technological:** cloud gaming; AI; brain-computer interfaces.
- **Economic:** platform economics; subscription models; monetising digital assets.
- **Environmental:** e-waste; the energy data centres burn.
- **Political:** online gambling law; who governs a metaverse; data privacy.

Five of those seemed to carry most of the weight to 2030:

1. **VR and AR.** Players want to be inside the game. EA's own developers describe how well the VR mission in Star Wars Battlefront was received (Clement, n.d.). AR, where the game comes into your room instead of you going into it, is the next step.
2. **AI.** The narrow kind, which does one task well, is already in EA's games: TRACAB, which EA bought in 2025, turns camera tracking of real matches into how players move on screen (Electronic Arts, 2025b). The general kind, as capable as a person across the board, is still some years out (Iqbal, 2024). I left anything beyond that out of a five-year view.
3. **Digital assets.** Tokens that prove you own a specific digital item (NFTs) make it possible to carry an item from one game or world to another (Sharma, 2025). If players ever build worlds where things of real value change hands, this gets big.
4. **Data privacy.** The more of you a platform holds, the more it has to protect, and laws like South Africa's POPIA set the bar (POPIA, n.d.).
5. **Metaverse governance.** A persistent shared space where your avatar carries your identity and money from one experience to the next (Tucci & Moore, 2024) needs rules, or people get hurt.

## Three horizons

The three horizons model asks where you are, where you're going, and what the messy middle looks like.

**Horizon 1, now.** Console and PC games, sold through platforms EA doesn't own (PlayStation, Xbox, Switch), with live-service revenue on top. The risk is being so good at the current model that you can't leave it: dependence on console makers who also sell their own games, and monetisation that a growing number of players and regulators object to.

**Horizon 3, where it could go.** EA as a platform rather than a publisher: adaptive, immersive worlds where players are co-authors rather than consumers, with AI as a creative partner. That is a different business with a different relationship to its customers, and it would need a different reputation than the one EA currently has.

**Horizon 2, the bridge.** Generative AI in the development pipeline, partnerships with cloud providers to host whatever comes next, and small pilots of shared worlds rather than one big launch. The thing that decides whether the bridge holds is trust: build it with players as stakeholders or watch it fizzle.

## Four futures

To stress-test that, I wrote four scenarios using Smart's version of Dator's four archetypes (Smart, n.d.): things continue, things get constrained, things collapse, things transform. I gave each a name a gamer would recognise.

| Future | Name | What it means for EA |
|---|---|---|
| Continuation | *Ultimate Franchise Mode* | EA keeps optimising the current model. Revenue holds; someone else takes the next leap. |
| Limits and discipline | *Code of Conduct* | Regulators move on AI, loot boxes and data. The business logic of live services has to change. |
| Decline and collapse | *Crash to Desktop* | A breakdown in trust, infrastructure or online access. The platform goes dark. |
| Transformation | *Respawn Protocol* | Players co-create in shared, augmented worlds. AI becomes a narrative partner. |

Any of the four could be the real Horizon 3. The useful question is which moves are sensible in all of them.

## What held up across all four

Five things did:

- **AI needs governance as much as it needs engineers.** An ethics board, investment in the toolchain, and retraining designers rather than replacing them.
- **Ownership and money in immersive worlds will be contested.** Better to enable a player economy and take a cut than to impose one.
- **Regulation is coming for the current revenue model.** Diversify away from loot boxes before being made to, and be transparent about data.
- **The talent mix has to change.** People who understand game design, machine learning and the ethics of data at the same time, plus an in-house foresight capability.
- **Platform risk grows.** Less dependence on consoles; more cross-platform and cloud-native.

From there, the preferred future: by 2030, EA makes original titles, empowers creator communities, has credible AI governance, and runs the most trusted immersive platforms in the industry. Four recommendations, one per area: an AI-enabled design studio open to user-generated content; a foresight-informed ethics board over all live services; a pilot of cross-game asset ownership on a secure ledger; and training leaders in futures thinking.

## What I got wrong

Two corrections.

I wrote that loot boxes had been outlawed in Japan, Belgium and the Netherlands. Only Belgium is right. Japan's consumer regulator banned one specific mechanic, "complete gacha", in 2012 and left ordinary randomised packs alone (Betable, 2012). In the Netherlands, the gambling regulator did fine EA over FIFA packs, but the country's highest administrative court overturned it in March 2022, ruling that opening packs is part of a game of skill, not a game of chance on its own (de Vrey, 2022). The regulatory future is still real; it's just less far along than I said.

I also implied EA had used AI-generated voices without actors' consent. The source I had was about the games industry's year-long voice-actor strike, which EA was party to, and which ended in July 2025 with an agreement requiring consent and disclosure before a performer's digital replica is used (Richardson, 2025). That's a fair basis for "AI needs governance." It isn't evidence of a specific act by EA, and I shouldn't have written it as if it were.

## Then someone bought the company

The buyout is the biggest thing my scan missed. A consortium of Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund, Silver Lake and Affinity Partners paid $210 a share in cash, and the deal closed on 4 August 2026 (Electronic Arts, 2025a; 2026). EA is private now.

What that does to the four futures, as best I can tell from outside:

- *Ultimate Franchise Mode* gets less likely, not more. The force that kept EA optimising was the quarterly earnings cycle. A private owner with very deep pockets doesn't have one, and the price paid only makes sense if the owners expect the company to grow into something bigger than it is.
- *Respawn Protocol* gets more fundable. The horizon 2 investments I listed, AI tooling, cloud partnerships, pilots that may not pay off for years, are exactly the kind a public company struggles to justify and a private one can simply make.
- *Code of Conduct* gets harder to watch. Public companies file annual reports and I read EA's closely for the earlier piece. Private ones don't. The transparency I recommended on data and monetisation is now voluntary, and the regulators are the only ones who can insist.
- *Crash to Desktop* is the one the buyout doesn't touch. Trust with players is earned in the games, not in the ownership structure.

## What I take from it

The exercise held up better than I expected, and not because the scenarios were clever. It held up because the robust moves, the ones that make sense in every future, don't depend on knowing which future arrives. Governance for AI, a monetisation model that doesn't need a loophole, less platform dependence: those were sensible in July 2025 under public ownership and they're sensible now under private ownership.

What changed is who gets to decide, and how much the rest of us will be able to see.
