In December 2024 I wrote a case study on Nayib Bukele, the president of El Salvador. I used him as an example of future-back thinking: deciding where you want to end up, then working backwards to what you have to do now.

Two years later I went back to it. This is what I argued, what has happened since, and what I would write differently. It is not a verdict on him. Salvadorans have given theirs at the ballot box and in the polls, human rights groups have given a very different one, and both are below.

## The country he inherited

El Salvador's civil war ended in 1992. From then until 2019 the presidency stayed with the two parties that came out of that war: ARENA on the right, then the FMLN on the left (Congressional Research Service, 2026).

It did not go well. Antonio Saca, president from 2004 to 2009, confessed to diverting more than $300 million of public money and was given ten years in prison. His successor, Mauricio Funes, left for Nicaragua in 2016, accused of corruption, and died there in 2025 (Al Jazeera, 2026).

Meanwhile the gangs took the streets. In 2015 the country recorded 6,656 murders, a rate of 106 for every 100,000 people (Associated Press, 2026).

## His path

Bukele was born in San Salvador in 1981. At 18 he left university to work in his family's businesses. In 2012 he was elected mayor of the small town of Nuevo Cuscatlán for the FMLN, and in 2015 mayor of the capital (Encyclopaedia Britannica, n.d.).

Then the routes started closing. The FMLN expelled him in 2017. He founded his own party, Nuevas Ideas, that October, and the electoral tribunal ruled it had been formed too late to stand in 2019. So he ran under the banner of a third party, GANA, and won the presidency in February 2019 with nearly 53% of the vote (Encyclopaedia Britannica, n.d.). He was the first candidate in thirty years to win without ARENA or the FMLN behind him (Congressional Research Service, 2026).

I drew his life against the country's politics and its murder rate. The height of each event is my own judgement of how good or bad it was for him, not a measurement.

## What I argued

Most planning is what Johnson and Suskewicz (2020) call present-forward: take today, improve it a little, repeat. Future-back runs the other way. You develop a vision of where you want to be, convert it into a strategy, then programme and carry it out.

Bukele looked to me like a textbook case. The destination stayed fixed and the route changed every time one closed: expelled from one party, he built another; blocked from using it, he borrowed a third.

His security policy had the same shape. Days after taking office he launched the Territorial Control Plan, announced from the start as seven phases, with the seventh held back in case the first six failed (Americas Society/Council of the Americas, 2020). The sixth, Integration, was launched in September 2023 (El Salvador in English, 2023).

I wrote that nothing seems to stop him once he has a vision in mind.

## What has happened since

**The numbers he is judged on kept improving.** The government counted 82 murders in 2025 (Associated Press, 2026). In a poll in May 2026, nearly 88% of Salvadorans approved of the job he was doing (Congressional Research Service, 2026). In the Global Organized Crime Index the country moved from 52nd most affected in 2023 to 77th in 2025 (Global Initiative Against Transnational Organized Crime, 2025).

**The emergency became permanent.** The state of exception declared in March 2022 suspends some constitutional rights. It has been renewed every month since, and by mid-2026 more than 92,000 people had been detained under it (Congressional Research Service, 2026). Bukele himself has acknowledged that at least 8,000 of them were innocent (EFE, 2026). One local group counts at least 570 deaths in prison, and a panel of international jurists reported in March 2026 that there were reasonable grounds to believe crimes against humanity were being committed (Congressional Research Service, 2026). Amnesty International (2026) reached a similar conclusion that July.

**The limits on his office were removed.** The constitution barred a president from serving two terms in a row. In 2021 his party used its new majority to replace five Supreme Court judges and the attorney general, and the reshaped court then ruled that he could stand again (Encyclopaedia Britannica, n.d.). He won in February 2024 with 84.7% of the vote. In 2025 the legislature amended the constitution to allow indefinite re-election and six-year terms (Congressional Research Service, 2026). In July 2026 his party nominated him for a third term, in an election due in February 2027. His answer to the critics is that most developed countries let their head of government be re-elected without limit and "nobody bats an eye" (Associated Press, 2026).

**Bitcoin was walked back.** El Salvador made it legal tender in 2021. In January 2025 the legislature reversed that, and the following month the IMF approved a $1.4 billion programme (Congressional Research Service, 2026).

**The economy is steady, not transformed.** Growth was 2.6% in 2024 and 3.7% in 2025. About a quarter of the population lives in poverty, and money sent home from abroad is worth roughly 27% of the economy (Congressional Research Service, 2026).

## What I got wrong, or left out

**I gave the costs one row in a table.** The mass detentions were already well documented when I wrote. I noted "growing humanitarian concern" and moved on to the results.

**I used bitcoin as proof of vision.** It stopped being legal tender about seven weeks after I finished writing.

**I gave him all the credit for the fall in murders.** My own chart shows the line peaking in 2015 and already falling when he took office in 2019; official figures put the rate at 106 in 2015 and 53 in 2018, the year before he became president (El Salvador in English, 2025). There is also the question of how the early fall on his watch came about. In December 2021 the US Treasury sanctioned two of his officials, saying they had negotiated a secret truce with the gangs. Bukele has always denied any such deal (Reuters, 2021). Meléndez-Sánchez and Vergara (2024) argue that the later crackdown worked partly because of the unintended effects of that pact, and that countries copying the model are likely to fail. And the official count itself is narrower than most countries': it leaves out deaths in prison, deaths involving the police and bodies found in unmarked graves (Congressional Research Service, 2026).

**I treated the second term as a footnote.** I wrote one line saying he was accused of running illegally. Since then the term limit has gone altogether.

**I leaned on weak sources.** A good part of the original rested on Wikipedia and on a news site that reports mostly from the government's side. This version is rebuilt on a research report for the US Congress, wire reporting, a peer-reviewed article and the organisations' own publications.

## What I would say now

The lens still explains how he got there. Fix the destination, stay loose about the route, and be willing to do what the people around you won't.

But future-back thinking is a method. It tells you how someone reaches the future they pictured. It has nothing to say about what that cost, who paid, or whether anyone can still tell them no.

In a company those questions are asked by a board, by customers and by competitors. In a country they are asked by courts, by term limits and by voters. Voters are still asking: there is an election in February 2027. The courts and the term limits are the parts that changed.

So the next time I use a strategy framework on a person, I will put three questions beside it. What did it cost? Who paid? Who can still stop it?
